Abstract

Time series from real-world systems are often accompanied by text, such as a report on an event that affects them. Two common tasks on these series are understanding, e.g., answering questions about a series, and forecasting its future values. Both tasks require reasoning over the series and the text together. Moreover, understanding outputs text while forecasting outputs a time series, so serving both requires a model that reads and generates both modalities. Large language models (LLMs) read time series as digit tokens and handle long series poorly. Time series foundation models (TSFMs) forecast well from large-scale pretraining but typically neither read nor generate text. Time series language models add a time series modality to the LLM but forecast on the LLM side, leaving the TSFM’s pretrained forecasting ability unused. We present TimeOmni-o1, which couples a pretrained TSFM and an LLM bidirectionally. In one direction, the TSFM encodes the series in compressed form for the LLM, which reasons over it together with the text. In the other, the LLM’s reasoning is fed back into the TSFM, which produces the forecast conditioned on it. One model architecture thus reads and generates both series and text, reasons in text, and draws on the TSFM’s pretrained forecasting ability. To train it, we build MMTR, a multimodal time series reasoning dataset with annotated reasoning traces, covering both tasks across 12 domains with 500k+ samples. On MMTR, TimeOmni-o1 outperforms existing models on both understanding and forecasting across most domains, as well as the LLM and the TSFM inside it when used individually. The gains carry over to three open benchmarks, where it outperforms the dedicated time series models.

Model

TimeOmni-o1 performs both the understanding and the forecasting task with one architecture (Figure 1). The input is a multivariate time series of $C$ channels, paired with a textual context: a question, background, or an event description. For understanding, the model generates a reasoning trace followed by a textual answer. For forecasting, the trace instead ends in a forecast instruction, and the output is a quantile forecast over the requested horizon, the window of future steps each sample specifies. The instruction states the window over which the event is expected to act, and the direction, shape and relative magnitude of its effect within that window.

Both task types share one workflow in which each component performs the function it was pretrained for: the LLM generates all text, and the TSFM encodes the time series and produces the forecast. The two components are coupled bidirectionally, with one pathway in each direction. On the series-to-LLM pathway, the TSFM maps the time series to patch representations, which are compressed into a bounded soft prompt that the LLM reads alongside the context to generate the trace and the answer or instruction. On the LLM-to-TSFM pathway, used for forecasting, the LLM’s hidden states are compressed and injected into the TSFM, which produces the forecast conditioned on them.

Series to LLM. To process long time series, the series-to-LLM pathway compresses the TSFM’s patch representations into a bounded soft prompt of local, global, and statistics tokens, which the LLM reads alongside the context (Figure 2, top). LLM to TSFM. The LLM-to-TSFM pathway compresses the LLM’s hidden states and injects them into every TSFM block through gated cross-attention, so the forecast is conditioned on the reasoning (Figure 2, bottom).

Dataset: MMTR

Table 1 collects the related multimodal time series corpora, and no corpus in it combines a reasoning trace on every sample, both task types and real-world time series. We therefore build MMTR, a multimodal time series reasoning dataset with annotated reasoning traces, covering both the understanding and the forecasting task. It holds 573,653 samples: 568,586 for the understanding task over 12 domains and 5,067 for the event-conditioned forecasting task over 5 domains.

Understanding task

The understanding task is built from six public reasoning-annotated corpora: the OpenTSLM chain-of-thought sets (Langer et al., 2026), ST-Bench (Ni et al., 2026), RATs40K (Yang et al., 2026), TSRBench (Zhou et al., 2026), TelecomTS (Feng et al., 2026) and HiTSR (Ding et al., 2026). They are unified into one schema of time series, question, reasoning trace and answer. The questions cover open question answering, multiple choice, classification, anomaly detection and diagnosis, and multi-step numerical calculation. The samples span 12 domains: physiology, telecommunications, urbanism, information technology, neuroscience, earth science, meteorology, economics, bioacoustics, manufacturing, energy, and synthetic data.

Forecasting task

The forecasting task is event-conditioned. Each sample pairs a time series history with a background and the description of an event (e.g., a heatwave alert, a holiday, a market event), and asks for a forecast over a horizon. The time series and their accompanying texts are collected from five public multimodal forecasting sources: CGTSF (Wang et al., 2025), Fidel-TS (Xu et al., 2025), MTBench (Chen et al., 2025), News Forecast (Wang et al., 2024) and Time-MMD (Liu et al., 2024). These sources contribute ten subsets in five domains: solar power, grid load, traffic flow, finance and climate. They carry no reasoning annotations, so we annotate every sample with Claude Sonnet 4.6 to add an event description condensed from the accompanying text, a reasoning trace, and a forecast instruction. The annotator writes the reasoning trace and the forecast instruction from the history and the event while seeing the future values, so the instruction matches the realized future, but it is prompted not to cite any specific future value. The window stated in the instruction, over which the event is expected to act, is the event region of interest (ROI). We record it as a separate field in each sample for ROI-weighted training and ROI evaluation. The subsets and the annotation protocol are detailed in Annotation and human review.

Annotation and human review

Annotation protocol. The 5,067 collected samples (4,630 training and 437 test) are annotated by an LLM annotator, Claude Sonnet 4.6. Annotation runs in two stages, separated by what each field is used for. The event description is a model input at test time, so the first stage must not depend on the future. In this stage the annotator receives a hand-written dataset background, the sample’s accompanying text and the timestamps of the history and the horizon; it receives neither the time series nor any future value. The accompanying text is the source’s own aligned text rather than retrieved material: weather forecasts and reports for the solar and load sets, aligned news for the finance and traffic sets, and drought reports for the climate set. Text dated inside the forecast horizon is marked, and the annotator is instructed to ignore it. The annotator returns the event description, condensed from the accompanying text without causal phrasing. The reasoning trace, the forecast instruction and the ROI are training targets and are written by the annotator in the second stage. The instruction names the ROI and states the direction, shape and relative magnitude of the effect in words. Here the annotator receives the background, the event description of the first stage as a fixed input, the window metadata, a rendering of the time series, and the future window as a timestamped value table. Three constraints are imposed on the second stage: the ROI lies inside the horizon, the trace uses the history and the event only, and no future value is cited. A schema check enforces the ROI bounds, and an automatic check flags any quoted future value.

Trace quality is checked by human review. Two independent reviewers rate 1,000 traces, sampled with stratification by task and domain, against the input time series, the context, and the annotated answer. A trace fails if its reasoning contradicts the time series, the context, or the final answer, or if it contains a factual error. On average, 93.3% of the understanding traces and 97.7% of the forecasting traces pass, and the two reviewers give the same pass/fail label for 96.6% of the traces. The review protocol and the per-task pass rates are given in the paper’s appendix.

Case studies

This section presents two understanding cases and two forecasting cases; the statistics over the full test sets are those of Section 6.3 of the paper. Baselines are scored leniently throughout: an output counts as correct if the ground-truth phrase appears anywhere in it, a bare option letter counts, and the last number of a numeric answer is taken. In every quoted trace, the cited values are approximate, because the LLM reads the time series through the soft prompt rather than as digits; what is checked against the raw time series is the structural judgment: how many events, how large, and which shape.

Understanding cases

U1 Counting drops (TSRBench, Earth science)

The question asks how many significant downward spikes in a 256-day soil-nutrient time series could critically impact plant growth; the answer is 3. The trace of TimeOmni-o1 sets a criterion first and then enumerates: it lists three drops of about 7 to 8 units with their days, and it names smaller declines that it rules out. Against the raw time series, exactly three one-day drops exceed 5 units (5.2, 5.6 and 8.8) and the fourth largest is 1.3. Every other model that produces a number is off by one (Table 3): the no-reasoning model and TimeOmni-1 answer 2, the fine-tuned LLM and TimeOmni answer 1, and Time-MQA detects only the first drop. The models that state a count directly undercount, whereas the trace counts the drops one by one against a stated criterion.

Question as posed
You are a time series analysis expert. This is a metric called Soil Nutrient Levels collected from Agriculture with length of 256:. In the context of Soil Nutrient Levels, considering the time series starts from the beginning of a growing season and each point represents a day, how many significant downward spikes in nutrient levels occurred that could critically impact plant growth?
Reasoning trace generated by TimeOmni-o1
Step 1 Analyzing task intent:  
[Judgment] Significant downward spikes  
[Description] The problem requires counting "significant downward spikes" in soil nutrient levels that could "critically impact plant growth." Keywords like "significant," "downward spikes," and "critically impact" indicate the need to identify abrupt, severe drops in nutrient levels rather than gradual trends or minor fluctuations.  

Step 2 Selecting task-relevant key patterns:  
[Judgment] Amplitude deviation; Rate of change; Threshold deviation  
[Description]  
- **Amplitude deviation**: Measures the magnitude of a drop relative to preceding values. Critical for identifying "significant" spikes.  
- **Rate of change**: Detects abrupt declines (spikes) versus gradual decreases.  
- **Threshold deviation**: Uses domain knowledge (e.g., nutrient levels dropping below agronomic safety thresholds) to define "critical" impact.  

Step 3 Analyzing time series samples using selected key patterns:
